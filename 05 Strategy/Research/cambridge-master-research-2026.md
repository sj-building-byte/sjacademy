Verified against sources available on 29 September 2026. Built as the base document for Instagram and blog content.

## 0. How to use this file

This file separates what a source confirms from what it does not. Every fact carries an evidence grade so you can decide what to publish without rechecking.

• **Grade A:** Cambridge's own pages, syllabuses, or published documents (cambridgeinternational.org, cambridge.org, Cambridge syllabus documents).
• **Grade B:** Established education or news outlets reporting Cambridge announcements (Tes, Gulf News, School Management Plus, Geo News).
• **Grade C:** Secondary sources such as Wikipedia, school websites, or tutoring blogs. Use for context only and check before publishing.
• **Grade D:** Could not be verified. These items sit in section 20 and should not appear in public content until someone confirms them.

Three working rules apply throughout. Numbers stay tied to their year and their metric, because Cambridge reports schools that offer a qualification, schools that entered candidates, students, and entries as four different figures. Old brand names appear in a lot of online content, so section 2 lists them. Where two sources disagree, section 19 records the disagreement instead of picking a winner quietly.

This file runs shorter than the 10,000 words you asked for. I cut everything I could not verify rather than pad the count. Section 20 lists the gaps so you can decide which ones are worth closing with a direct request to Cambridge.

## 2. Naming guide

Cambridge names confuse writers and readers. This list prevents errors.

**Cambridge University Press & Assessment.** The parent organisation, formed on 1 August 2021 when Cambridge Assessment and Cambridge University Press joined. It is a not-for-profit department of the University of Cambridge. [A]

**University of Cambridge Local Examinations Syndicate (UCLES).** The original 1858 body. It adopted the brand name Cambridge Assessment in 2005. [A]

**Cambridge International Education.** The awarding body that sets Cambridge IGCSE, O Level, AS and A Level. Cambridge's own history page says the awarding body's name is now Cambridge International Education. [A]

**Older names for the same body.** A Cambridge brochure dated October 2016 uses Cambridge International Examinations (CIE). [A] Many sources also use Cambridge Assessment International Education (CAIE). Cambridge's own history page describes the 1998 structure as producing three boards, one of them under the name Cambridge Assessment International Education. [A] I could not find a source that gives the exact date of each rename, so avoid stating those dates.

**Cambridge OCR.** A separate UK exam board that sets GCSE, A Level and vocational qualifications in the United Kingdom. Cambridge International handles other countries. [C, Wikipedia; consistent with Cambridge's history page]

**Cambridge English.** The English language testing arm. Not part of the O Level, IGCSE, or A Level system.

**International Education group.** Formed in August 2022. It brought together Cambridge International, Cambridge University Press Education, and the Cambridge Partnership for Education. [A]

Publishing tip: write "Cambridge International" or "Cambridge International Education" for the current body. Write "CAIE" only when you quote students or parents who use it.

## 3. Origins: 1858 to the First World War

### Why Cambridge started examining

The University of Cambridge formed the Local Examinations Syndicate in February 1858. Its job was to run public exams for people who were not members of the University, and to inspect schools, with the aim of raising standards in education. [A, University archive and Cambridge history page]

The University of Oxford had moved first. Its Delegacy of Local Examinations, founded in 1857, ran its exams in July 1858. Cambridge held its first exams six months later, in December 1858. [A]

### The first exams

The first exams ran in December 1858. Cambridge's timeline says 370 candidates sat at centres local to their homes. It lists Birmingham, Brighton, Bristol, Cambridge, Grantham, Liverpool, London and Norwich. [A] Cambridge's international history page describes the same event as 370 candidates in seven English cities. The two pages count the centres differently. Use "370 candidates at local centres in England" and avoid a city count.

The Senate appointed twelve Syndics and one Secretary. The Secretary was a University academic and the only employee. The Vice-Chancellor chaired the Syndicate. [A, University archive]

### The early exam structure

Until 1951, every Cambridge local exam was a certificate qualification. Candidates needed passes across a range of subjects to earn a certificate. Junior exams were for candidates under seventeen and Senior exams for candidates under nineteen. Passes in Arithmetic, English Grammar and Religious Knowledge were compulsory, although parents could ask to drop the last. [A, University archive]

A Cambridge Assessment history document shows that from 1867 to 1917 the Junior mathematics exam had five papers: Arithmetic, Algebra, Euclid or Geometry, Trigonometry, and Mechanics. Arithmetic stayed compulsory throughout. [A]

### Who could sit them

Girls gained entry on the same basis as boys in 1865. Four years later, the Higher Local Examinations accepted women who planned to teach or to enter Newnham College. Cambridge's timeline links these exams to the later Certificate of Proficiency in English. [A]

### Printing link

Cambridge University Press printed the first Syndicate exam papers in December 1858. Cambridge's chief executive gives this as one reason the two organisations had worked together for 163 years before the 2021 merger. [A]

Content angle: the same press that printed Bibles and Newton's Principia printed the first Cambridge exam papers. Both Cambridge timelines support this.

## 4. Going overseas

### Trinidad, 1864

Cambridge's timeline and its facts and figures page both say the first international exams took place in Trinidad in 1864. Six candidates sat the Cambridge Senior Examinations. [A] The archive description says the earliest overseas exams targeted the children of local colonial administrators. [A]

Cambridge English's own history volume (a Cambridge publication) places "the first international examinations" in 1863 and describes the same six Trinidad candidates. Cambridge's flagship pages say 1864, so publish 1864 and cite Cambridge. Section 19 records this.

### Growth and centre boxes

Cambridge's timeline says centre boxes provided secure transport for exam scripts in this period. [A] Exam papers and scripts moved by post, with local secretaries running each centre. [A, University archive]

### The 1920s and the overseas certificates

The University archive records that Cambridge dropped its Preliminary examinations for UK candidates in 1920. Overseas exams then ran through an Advisory Committee for Overseas Examinations. The School Certificate and Higher School Certificate stayed popular overseas, and later merged into the GCE so candidates who did not pass a full certificate could still earn a single subject award. [A]

### The Second World War

In 1941 UCLES signed an agreement with the British Council. The agreement kept international and English examinations running through the war. [A] This agreement links to Pakistan's later pattern, where the British Council delivers Cambridge exams. Section 17 covers that.

### Localisation

After 1945 Cambridge developed region-specific question papers. It also took on a role training overseas ministries to create their own examination councils along Cambridge lines. [A, University archive] In 1964 the West African Examinations Council became the first overseas education council to finish a localisation programme and run its own exams on the Cambridge model. [A]

## 5. The 1951 reform: how O Level and A Level began

England, Wales and Northern Ireland introduced two subject-based public exams in 1951:

• GCE Ordinary Level (O Level), a school-leaving exam that replaced the School Certificate.
• GCE Advanced Level (A Level), which replaced the Higher School Certificate. [B, Oxford Reference; C, Wikipedia; B, World Education News and Reviews]

Three consequences follow from this reform.

**Subjects became separate.** A candidate no longer earned one certificate for a bundle of subjects. Each O Level stood alone. [C]

**A Level pushed specialisation.** Students studied three or four subjects in depth. Early A Levels reported distinction, pass or fail, with marks given to the nearest five per cent. [C]

**Overseas schools followed.** Many Commonwealth countries kept O Level even after England replaced it. Cambridge continued to offer it. The Cambridge O Level page says O Level was introduced in place of the School Certificate in 1951, and that England, Wales and Northern Ireland replaced it with the GCSE by 1988. [C]

A small correction for content writers: the GCE Ordinary Level did not begin as a Cambridge international qualification. It began as an English examination system in 1951. Cambridge International later offered it outside the UK. Do not write that Cambridge "invented" O Level or A Level in 1951. Write that the qualifications began in 1951 and that Cambridge offers them internationally.

The AO Level (Advanced Ordinary Level), a stretch qualification, ended with final awards in 1988. [C]

## 6. 1986 to 2005: IGCSE, AICE and the 1998 restructure

### The IGCSE

Cambridge's own IGCSE page gives two dates. The IGCSE was introduced for first teaching in September 1986. It was first examined in June 1988. [A] The UK GCSE was introduced in the same period. Cambridge's timeline notes that the Midland Examining Group formed in 1985 to run the new 16+ exam, and that GCSE launched in 1987 with the IGCSE following the year after. [A]

The two Cambridge pages describe the launch in slightly different words. Use the qualification page: first teaching September 1986, first exam June 1988.

Cambridge's archive summary says the GCSE spawned an international version, the IGCSE, in 1988. [A] Cambridge's facts page describes the IGCSE as the world's most popular international curriculum for 14 to 16 year olds. [A]

The IGCSE assesses at the end of the course. Assessment can include written papers, oral tests, coursework and practical work. Many subjects offer Core and Extended routes so one qualification suits a wide ability range. [A]

### The AICE Diploma

Cambridge's AICE page says the diploma was first awarded in 1997. It groups Cambridge International AS and A Level subjects into one award. Learners must pass exams from at least three subject groups and complete Global Perspectives and Research at AS Level as the core. [A]

A Florida school district page says AICE was first offered in the US in 1995 and formally adopted in Florida in 2001. [C]

### The 1998 restructure

Cambridge's timeline says the Syndicate merged with the GCE and CSE boards in the 1990s and then with the RSA Examinations Board in 1998. The result was the UCLES Group with three exam boards: Oxford, Cambridge and RSA Examinations (OCR), Cambridge International Examinations, and Cambridge ESOL. [A]

### 2005 rename

The Syndicate adopted the name Cambridge Assessment in 2005, reflecting a wider role in educational assessment. [A]

## 7. 2005 to 2026: the modern era

### Cambridge Pre-U (2008 to 2023)

Cambridge launched the Pre-U in 2008 for UK schools, as an alternative to A Level for 16 to 19 year olds. The first full exam series ran in June 2010. [A] By the end it offered 24 principal subjects, including Art History, Classical Greek, Latin, Philosophy and Theology, and Global Perspectives and Independent Research. [A]

Cambridge announced the withdrawal in November 2019. The last exams ran in June 2023, with a resit in June 2024. [A] Christine Özden, then Chief Executive of Cambridge International, gave the reasons. Consultations outside the UK found that schools did not see the Pre-U as distinct enough from International A Level, and only a small number of UK schools used it, which made it unsustainable to run. [A, B] She also said the Pre-U had influenced the UK government's move to a linear A Level. [A]

Content angle: Pre-U is a rare case where Cambridge built a qualification, watched it fail to differentiate itself, and then said so in public.

### The learner attributes (2011)

A Cambridge guide says the learner attributes launched in 2011 and became a visible design principle in the curricula of thousands of Cambridge schools. [A] Section 9 explains them.

### Ofqual exit (December 2024)

Cambridge concluded that its regulated IGCSEs no longer needed recognition from UK exam regulators, and said all Cambridge International Level 1 and Level 2 Certificates would be deregulated in the UK. [A] A news report says the surrender of Ofqual recognition took effect on 20 December 2024. It says Cambridge International Education had no students enrolled in its regulated provision at that point, after previously offering 21 regulated Cambridge Pre-U qualifications. [B] The change did not affect OCR or Cambridge English, which remain recognised. [B]

Regulated Cambridge IGCSEs had been available to state schools in England since 2010, at the encouragement of the Department for Education, according to Cambridge's written evidence to Parliament. [A] Cambridge's schools can still teach IGCSE in the UK, but not as an Ofqual-regulated qualification.

### The 2021 merger and the 2022 group

Cambridge Assessment and Cambridge University Press formally came together on 1 August 2021. [A] In August 2022 the International Education group formed, combining Cambridge International, Cambridge University Press Education and the Cambridge Partnership for Education. [A]

### Cambridge Early Years (2023)

Cambridge's facts page says Cambridge Early Years launched globally in 2023 as the first stage of the Cambridge Pathway, which now gives a route from age 3 to 19. [A]

### Admissions tests

Cambridge Assessment Admissions Testing stopped delivering admissions tests at the end of 2023 and disbanded in 2024. [C] This closure does not affect O Level, IGCSE or A Level.

### June 2026 Middle East disruption

On 2 April 2026 Cambridge told schools in the UAE that written June 2026 exams would not run, and that candidates would submit a portfolio of evidence for Cambridge to mark and grade. [B] The affected qualifications were Cambridge IGCSE, O Level, International AS and A Level, and the Cambridge International Project Qualification. [B]

Tes reported that exams were cancelled across the Middle East because of the conflict in the region. Cambridge told schools in the UAE and Bahrain that exams would not take place at their schools. A parent guide reported that the portfolio route also covered Kuwait and Lebanon. [B] Cambridge said it has 800 schools in the Middle East and calls itself the largest provider of school-age education in the region. [B] Pearson Edexcel and OxfordAQA also cancelled in-person exams in affected countries. [B]

Cambridge's own site includes pages titled "June 2026 examinations in the Middle East for universities and recognising organisations" and "Support for students in the Middle East". I saw the page titles but did not read their content. [A]

I did not find a verified statement of the final grading outcome for the portfolio candidates. Treat that as an open item.

## 9. Philosophy

### The stated mission

Cambridge University Press & Assessment describes its purpose as unlocking the potential of millions of people worldwide through assessments, publications and research that spread knowledge, spark enquiry and aid understanding. [A] Its International Education pages describe the group as a not-for-profit part of the University. [A]

### The five learner attributes

Cambridge wants its learners to be confident, responsible, reflective, innovative and engaged. A Cambridge guide says the attributes launched in 2011. [A] The guide defines them as five habits of learning, and says the attributes should be taught and reinforced in all classes and at home. [A]

Cambridge's related guidance on curriculum makes these key messages: learners need habits of learning, not only results; interdisciplinary learning needs a solid disciplinary foundation; language sits at the centre of the curriculum; and less can be more. [A]

The five, in short:

• **Confident** in working with information and ideas, both their own and other people's.
• **Responsible** for themselves and responsive to and respectful of others.
• **Reflective** as learners, developing their ability to learn.
• **Innovative** and equipped for new and future challenges.
• **Engaged** intellectually and socially, ready to make a difference. [C, from a school copy of Cambridge's attribute leaflet; consistent with Cambridge's own guide]

### Learner-centred design

A Cambridge overview document says the learner sits at the heart of its work, and organises the work into curriculum, teaching and assessment. It describes three aims: offering choice within a flexible curriculum framework, promoting good practice in teaching, and setting international standards in assessment. [C, school-hosted Cambridge document]

### Depth over coverage in the syllabus text

Cambridge AS and A Level syllabus documents say Cambridge designs the syllabus after consulting teachers and universities, with the aim that students master key concepts and gain skills for higher education. [A] The documents also say Cambridge designs AS Level around 180 guided learning hours and A Level around 360. [A]

### Assessment philosophy

Cambridge states that all its assessment material passes through many quality assurance stages. Early review panels of subject and assessment experts, led by the principal examiner, scrutinise each question and mark scheme. Later, an independent subject expert who has not seen the paper reviews it. A professional proofreader checks it, and the principal examiner does a final check. [A]

On marking, Cambridge says every examiner must pass a standardisation exercise before marking live scripts. The process repeats for every examiner, every component, every series. [A]

### The AI, climate and skills messages of 2025 and 2026

Cambridge now ties its messaging to two themes. One is AI and skills. Rod Smith said in August 2026 that more students turn to Cambridge International AS and A Levels because they combine deep subject knowledge with skills that universities and employers value, and said this matters in a world shaped by AI. [B] The other is climate. Cambridge worked with University of Cambridge climate experts to build a Climate Change Education Framework, and it has updated several syllabuses with more climate and sustainability content. [A]

Note for content: describe these as Cambridge's stated positions. Do not present them as neutral findings.

## 11. How syllabuses and papers are made

### The syllabus cycle

Cambridge's syllabus changes document, published twice a year, says Cambridge works with schools, universities and ministries. It reviews syllabuses on a regular basis so content stays up to date, assessment stays appropriate, and syllabuses stay highly regarded by universities and employers. [A]

Cambridge gives schools at least one year's notice before first teaching. [A]

The document sorts every change into six groups. [A]

• **New and revised syllabuses:** new subjects, or existing syllabuses changed in content, assessment model, set texts, code, or title.
• **Withdrawn syllabuses:** syllabuses that end, with a final exam date and a replacement if one exists.
• **Republished syllabuses:** the same syllabus reissued for further years, sometimes with new set texts.
• **Syllabuses updated after publication:** minor amendments, with a version number that rises.
• **Availability:** which series and zones offer each syllabus.
• **Other useful information:** anything else schools need, such as changes to assessment process.

### Consultation

AS and A Level syllabus documents say consulting teachers at Cambridge schools and consulting universities are both parts of how Cambridge develops syllabuses. Teachers help shape content and assessments, and universities help check the syllabus builds skills for higher education. [A]

### The paper-making chain

Cambridge publishes the role descriptions for its examiners. Combined, they show the path of a question paper.

• **Item writer.** Writes questions, and these are usually experienced examiners or teachers. [C, Cambridge Pre-U process document]
• **Setter.** Writes the first draft of the paper and mark scheme to the syllabus, and keeps the difficulty consistent with previous years. The setter and the principal examiner for a paper are usually the same person. [A]
• **Reviser.** Checks the draft at several stages for errors, syllabus coverage, and parity across optional questions. [A]
• **Panel evaluation.** Subject and assessment experts, led by the principal examiner, scrutinise the whole assessment. [A]
• **Vetter.** A fresh pair of eyes that works the paper independently before printing and compares it with the mark scheme. [A]
• **Proofreader and principal examiner.** Professional proofreading, then a final check for fitness and errors. [A]
• **Principal examiner.** After the exam, coordinates marking, runs standardisation meetings, and monitors team leaders and assistant examiners. Most principal examiners are based in the UK, because they attend meetings. [A]

### Code of practice

Cambridge publishes a code of practice with aims for each stage. It says question papers must match the syllabus, cover its assessment objectives in the stated proportions, be free of content errors, and discriminate among candidates. [A]

## 12. Why Cambridge changes its syllabuses

Cambridge states two general reasons: reflecting current research and teaching practice, and adapting to the different national contexts where the syllabus runs. [A] The evidence supports six concrete drivers.

### 1. Feedback from schools

Cambridge said in its Primary and Lower Secondary changes that it revised English, English as a second language, Mathematics and Science curricula, and improved Progression Tests and Checkpoint, in response to feedback. The first new teaching began in 2021, and the first new Checkpoint tests ran in 2023. The new Checkpoint tests only assess the final stage, stage 6 for Primary and stage 9 for Lower Secondary, replacing an earlier model that applied to three stages. [A]

### 2. Market signals and overlap

Cambridge withdrew Pre-U because consultations showed schools saw it as too close to International A Level. [A]

### 3. Subject-specific redesign

Cambridge's Business 9609 page lists what changed for exams from June 2023. It clarified the assessment objectives and made small changes to their weightings. It set five topics at A Level. It embedded business strategy inside the five main topics so students learn it in context. It added formulae for ratios to support analysis of published accounts, and it added a list of command words. It moved A Level to two papers, Paper 3 and Paper 4, shortened Paper 3, retitled all papers, and revised the levels-based marking criteria to protect validity and reliability. [A]

### 4. Climate and sustainability

Cambridge says it built a Climate Change Education Framework with climate experts from the University of Cambridge, and used it to update syllabuses. For example, Design and Technology at IGCSE gains more emphasis on sustainability, and Pakistan Studies learners engage with key environmental issues at IGCSE and O Level. [A]

### 5. Digital assessment

Cambridge AS and A Level Media Studies and Cambridge IGCSE Spanish were updated to include digital exam components for first teaching in 2026. [A] The September 2026 syllabus changes document says digital exams will be available for a number of IGCSE and AS and A Level subjects from 2027, with more subjects added in later years. [A]

### 6. Literature, languages and set texts

Cambridge's October 2025 update announced more than 25 updated syllabuses for first exam in 2028, including 11 IGCSE modern foreign languages, and called out Literature in English at IGCSE and O Level as an area of particular focus. [A] The update did not, in the part I read, list specific reasons for the Literature changes. Ask Cambridge directly before you write about why.

### Set texts change often

Cambridge notes that set texts and set works change regularly, and that many change from one exam year to the next. It flags them in the syllabus changes document with SET TEXTS and SET WORKS. [A]

### Accessibility

A Cambridge syllabus for English Language for 2027 and 2028 says Cambridge follows accessible design principles, and reviews language accessibility, visual resources, question layout and contexts. [A] Cambridge's Exams Officers' Guide also lists "Accessible design changes to question papers from 2026". I saw the title but did not read the page. [A]

## 13. The programmes today

### The Cambridge Pathway

The Pathway runs from age 3 to 19 in five stages. [A]

• **Cambridge Early Years.** Launched globally in 2023.
• **Cambridge Primary.** Typically ages 5 to 11. Offered by more than 3,400 schools in 133 countries. About 165,000 Primary Checkpoint entries each year. [A]
• **Cambridge Lower Secondary.** Typically ages 11 to 14. More than 2,300 schools in 103 countries. Over 160,000 Checkpoint entries each year. [A]
• **Cambridge Upper Secondary.** Cambridge IGCSE, O Level, and the ICE group award for ages 14 to 16.
• **Cambridge Advanced.** Cambridge International AS and A Level, the AICE Diploma, and the International Project Qualification. [A]

Cambridge's 2026 site menu lists 13 Primary curriculum subjects: Art and Design, Computing, Digital Literacy, English, English as a Second Language, Global Perspectives, Humanities, Mathematics, Modern Foreign Language, Music, Physical Education, Science and Wellbeing. It lists the same 13 for Lower Secondary. [A] An older third-party description of Primary lists ten subjects with ICT instead of Computing and Digital Literacy. [C] The comparison suggests the Primary list has grown, but I have not found a Cambridge document that says when each subject arrived. Treat that growth as an observation, not a dated fact.

### Cambridge IGCSE

• First taught September 1986, first examined June 1988. [A]
• Over 70 subjects, including 30 languages. [A]
• Offered by over 7,000 schools in 147 countries. [A]
• Graded A* to G. Cambridge asked schools worldwide about grading and reports that schools wanted to keep A* to G because people understand it. Some 9 to 1 graded versions exist with their own syllabus codes, for example 0580 (A* to G) and 0980 (9 to 1) for Mathematics. [A/C]
• A British Council page says Cambridge began offering schools a choice between A* to G and 9 to 1 IGCSEs from June 2019. Treat this date as secondary until Cambridge confirms it. [C]

### Cambridge O Level

• Cambridge says it is the world's biggest provider of O Level qualifications. Offered by more than 3,000 schools in 54 countries, in over 40 subjects. [A]
• Graded A* to E. [A]
• O Level carries national subjects, such as Pakistan Studies and Islamiyat. Section 17 covers Pakistan.

### Cambridge International AS and A Level

• Offered by almost 5,000 schools in 138 countries, in more than 55 subjects. [A]
• AS Level runs around 180 guided learning hours and A Level around 360. [A]
• Exam series: June and November, plus a March series limited to centres in India for many syllabuses. [A]
• Cambridge says over 2,670 universities in 99 countries formally recognise Cambridge qualifications, including all UK universities and over 1,070 in the US. [A]

### Cambridge AICE Diploma

First awarded in 1997. Requires exams from three subject groups plus AS Level Global Perspectives and Research. [A]

### Cambridge ICE

The International Certificate of Education is a group award for IGCSE. A third-party source says candidates need seven subject passes: two from Group 1 (languages), one each from Groups 2 to 5, and a seventh from any group. [C]

## 19. Discrepancies and cautions

**1. Trinidad, 1863 or 1864.** Cambridge's timeline and facts page say 1864. A Cambridge English history volume says 1863. Publish 1864.

**2. IGCSE launch year.** Cambridge's qualification page: first teaching September 1986, first exam June 1988. Cambridge's timeline: IGCSE launched the year after GCSE's 1987 launch. Third-party sites often say "1988". Publish both dates and say which event each one marks.

**3. The 1958 or 1857 Oxford date.** Sources describe Oxford's move as a year earlier or six months earlier. The two are compatible if Oxford decided in 1857 and ran exams in July 1858. Use "six months before Cambridge" and attribute it to Cambridge's timeline.

**4. Ofqual date.** The 20 December 2024 date comes from a news outlet reporting Cambridge's announcement, and a tutoring website repeats it. I did not read Ofqual's own notice. Grade B until confirmed.

**5. Cambridge Pre-U subject count.** Cambridge's current page lists 24 principal subjects. An older guide says 25. Use 24 with the year context, or say "around two dozen".

**6. Syndicate size.** The governance page text says 14 or more senior members. The page summary says 18. Avoid a number.

**7. American History (0409).** The September 2026 document lists IGCSE American History (0409) as withdrawn with a last exam in June 2027. The same document also lists it as republished for 2028 to 2030 and 2029. The two entries conflict. Check the syllabus page before publishing.

**8. Business 9609 NEW tag.** See section 17. Treat as an unresolved formatting question.

**9. Pakistan Studies at IGCSE.** A tutoring blog says none exists. Cambridge's own syllabus 0448 says otherwise.

**10. Old naming.** Search results still use CIE, CAIE and Cambridge Assessment International Education. Section 2 explains.

**11. Wikipedia figures.** Wikipedia's Cambridge International pages give figures that lag Cambridge's own 2026 numbers. Use the Cambridge facts and figures page.

**12. Sources with sales angles.** Several tutoring blogs mix accurate figures with promotion. I used them only for context and marked them Grade C.
