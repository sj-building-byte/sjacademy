# Price elasticity of demand (about 42 seconds): the one to record

Talk like you're explaining it to a student after class: relaxed, curious, a little smile. Pause briefly between paragraphs so I can cut in graphics. If you stumble, pause and repeat the whole sentence.

---

Why can petrol go up in price and people still buy it, but if your local burger place does the same, everyone leaves?

That's price elasticity of demand. It simply means how much people react when the price changes.

Burgers have easy alternatives. There's another place right next door, so people just walk away. That's elastic demand.

Petrol is something people need to get to work or school, and there's no real alternative, so they keep buying. That's inelastic demand.

That's why burger places fight with deals and discounts, while petrol prices can rise without sales falling much.

So before any business touches its price, it asks one question: can my customers easily go somewhere else?

If they can, be careful. If they can't, that's real pricing power.

---

## Accuracy check
- PED measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. "How much people react when the price changes" is a fair plain-English version.
- Elastic: plenty of close substitutes (one burger place versus the next). Inelastic: a necessity with few substitutes (petrol, in the short run).
- Inelastic demand means a price rise reduces sales only a little; elastic demand is why firms compete on price with deals.
- The closing question tests for substitutes, the main factor that determines PED. "Pricing power" (being able to raise the price without losing many sales) follows from inelastic demand.
