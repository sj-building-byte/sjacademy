# Explainer: Gravity (draft script v1)

- Audience: anyone, O Level and up. Silly simple: one idea per line, no formulas except one number.
- Length: about 70 seconds spoken. 9:16. English only.
- Voice: AI voice (Kokoro), to be chosen from `voice-auditions/`. Music: none under the explanation (sound playbook). Desk sounds and playful effects allowed.
- Visual direction: goes beyond the usual brand look. Bold illustrated scenes, a cartoon Earth with a face, a hand-drawn pen, a hammer and a feather, the Moon, Newton's cannonball. Brand colours, fonts and the logo still frame it.

| # | Voice line | Visual idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Drop a pen. It falls. Every single time. | A hand lets go of an SJ pen. It drops and bounces with a desk "tick". Freeze frame. |
| 2 | But why down? Why not sideways, or up? | The pen tries to float up and sideways, cartoon style, then gets yanked back down. |
| 3 | Here's the secret. Everything with mass pulls on everything else. | Two objects (a phone and a person) with little arrows pulling towards each other. |
| 4 | You pull on your phone, and your phone pulls on you. It's just a very, very tiny pull. | A tiny tug-of-war rope between a phone and a stick figure. A magnifying glass shows a microscopic arrow. |
| 5 | But Earth is huge. About six million billion billion kilograms. | Zoom out to a cartoon Earth. The number counts up as zeros pile on: 6,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg. |
| 6 | So Earth's pull wins. It tugs everything towards its centre. That's gravity. | Arrows from people all over the globe point to the centre. Big word: GRAVITY. |
| 7 | Now the weird part. Drop a heavy ball and a light ball together. They land together. | Split screen: a bowling ball and a tennis ball fall side by side and land at the same instant. Stamp. |
| 8 | Gravity speeds everything up by the same amount. About 9.8 metres per second, every second. | A speedometer on the falling ball ticks up: 9.8, 19.6, 29.4 m/s, one per second. |
| 9 | So why does a feather float down slowly? Air gets in the way. | A feather wobbles down, air "particles" bumping into it. |
| 10 | In 1971, an astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon, where there's no air. They hit the ground at the same time. | A Moon scene: hammer and feather fall together and land together. Dust puff. |
| 11 | The Moon is smaller, so its gravity is weaker. You'd weigh about one sixth of what you weigh here. | The same person stands on a force meter. On Earth it reads about 600 N; on the Moon it drops to about 100 N (see note). |
| 12 | Your mass doesn't change. Only your weight does. | Same stick figure, same size, labels: mass the same, weight smaller. |
| 13 | So next time you drop something, that's Earth pulling it in. | Back to the pen falling. The cartoon Earth winks. |
| 14 | (end card) | Logo, "Follow for more simple science", @sjacademypk, sonic logo. |

## Accuracy check (00 Start Here/ACCURACY.md)
- Every object with mass attracts every other (Newton's law of gravitation). Correct.
- Earth's mass is 5.97 × 10^24 kg, which is about 6 × 10^24, "six million billion billion". Correct: 10^6 × 10^9 × 10^9 = 10^24.
- Free-fall acceleration near Earth's surface is 9.8 m/s² (9.81). After 1 s, 2 s and 3 s the speed is 9.8, 19.6 and 29.4 m/s, ignoring air resistance. Correct.
- Heavy and light objects land together **if air resistance is negligible**. The script says this through the feather line before claiming it in general. A bowling ball and a tennis ball dropped from a small height land at almost the same time in air too.
- The Apollo 15 hammer and feather drop: astronaut David Scott, 2 August 1971, on the Moon. Correct.
- Moon surface gravity is about 1.62 m/s², roughly 1/6 of Earth's. Correct.
- Weight changes and mass does not. Correct.
- **Note for line 11:** a bathroom scale shows weight but is labelled in kg. To avoid confusing mass and weight, the visual should show weight in newtons (for example 600 N on Earth, about 100 N on the Moon for a 60 kg person: 60 × 9.8 = 588 N ≈ 600 N; 60 × 1.62 = 97 N ≈ 100 N), or no number at all.
