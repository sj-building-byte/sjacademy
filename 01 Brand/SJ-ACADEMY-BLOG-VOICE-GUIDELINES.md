# SJ Academy blog voice guidelines

**Version:** 1.3, October 2026
**Status:** Draft for approval. Version 1.3 is re-cut against four pieces the owner wrote himself (section 2). The sample passages in sections 8, 9, 15 and 16 are still new writing and need his sign-off.
**Owner:** Sunyyan Junaid
**Applies to:** every post on the sjacademy.pk blog, written by a person or drafted by an AI model.
**Sits under:** `32-SJ-ACADEMY-WEB-PLAYBOOK.md`. That guide covers design, facts, consent and page copy. This one covers how blog posts sound.

**Changed in 1.1:** humour is general and relatable, with no named place, organisation or person as a target. Added Gen Z awareness (section 7) and entertainment posts in the BuzzFeed style (section 8).

**Changed in 1.3:** the voice is rebuilt on the owner's own writing. New section 2 records his signature moves. The humour trait changes from "dry" to "funny": warmer, more exaggerated, more self-mocking than the earlier drafts assumed. Exclamation marks are allowed within a limit. First-person buying guides, the straight-faced piece and a corrections rule are added.

**Changed in 1.2:** emoji allowed where they add value (section 10). Karachi and Pakistan flavour added (section 7). Entertainment posts are for students only, at one for every five guides (section 9).

**How to use it**

- Writing with an AI model: paste section 18 into the prompt, then edit the draft with section 19.
- Writing or editing yourself: sections 2, 3, 7 and 19 are enough once you know the voice.

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

The blog sounds like a teacher who is good company. He gives you the verdict first, tells you how he got there in the first person, exaggerates for effect, laughs at himself before anyone else, and never makes you feel small. He has marked the paper and knows why the marks were lost, and he would rather show you than lecture you. The humour is the kind a whole classroom laughs at together. Nobody is the target.

---

## 2. Where this voice comes from

Four pieces written entirely by the owner were studied: a long first-person laptop review, two earlier tech blog posts (one a straight-faced satire) and a public apology written for a company blog. They are old and about other subjects. The moves below carry straight over.

### Signature moves

| Move | From his own writing | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Verdict first** | "Here's the bottom-line at the top" and "Depends. But the answer is MacBook Air." | Respects the reader's time. Kills the hedge in the next breath |
| **Confession, then the real answer** | "Because I have zero will power. And I'm impatient. But on a serious note..." | The joke lands, then the substance follows straight away |
| **The aside in brackets** | "(I'm a fanboy, not a fanatic)", "(unintended pun)" | A second, quieter voice commenting on the first |
| **Exaggeration** | "You can land an airplane on it", "designing rockets on your laptop" | Makes a plain point memorable |
| **Things that talk back** | A slow laptop that says "stop, I'm not here to fulfill your dreams :)". A device that "doesn't have my respect" | Gives objects a personality |
| **Staccato** | "That's. About. It." and "In 2021. On a $1300 machine." | A full stop as a drum beat, for the one point that deserves it |
| **The run-on, then the brake** | One long breathless sentence, followed by "But, I digress." | The length is the joke. The short line resets |
| **Self as the average person** | "the average user like me", "IF I ever learned to edit well" | Never claims to be above the reader |
| **Honest shrug** | "Honestly, I don't have much to write here." | Says so when there is nothing to say, instead of padding |
| **Reasons as a story** | "Why I was resisting", then "Why I finally caved in" | Takes the reader through a real decision, doubts included |
| **The tally** | "Great keyboard - Check. Great display - Check." | A rhythmic close that sums up without a summary paragraph |
| **Straight-faced praise** | A whole article solemnly arguing that a picture book "reinvented the book" | Satire by pretending to agree |
| **Owning a mistake** | "We weren't thinking at all (unfortunately)" and "No hard feelings, though. Our mistake was bigger" | No spin, no excuses, still human |

### What the old pieces do that the blog will not

These are habits of the time and the subject, or things the owner has since ruled out. They are listed so nobody copies them from the originals.

- **Buzzwords.** The old pieces use "game-changer", "seamless" and "skyrocketed". His rule now is no buzzwords, so they stay on the banned list.
- **Named targets.** The satire names a company and its executives. His rule now: no named place, organisation or person as a target. Keep the straight-faced method, change what it points at.
- **Favourite words on repeat.** "Insane", "hands down" and "seamless" each appear several times in one piece. One use per post.
- **Long paragraphs.** Several run past 100 words. The blog keeps to one to three sentences.
- **Exclamation marks in every section.** The blog keeps a few (section 10).
- **American spelling.** The old pieces use it. The site uses British spelling, to match Cambridge and the rest of the site. This is a call made in this guide and can be reversed.
- **Mild swearing** ("damn", "dammit"). Left out here, because the readers include 15-year-olds and their parents. Also a call made in this guide.

## 3. Five traits

| Trait | It is | It is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight** | The answer first. A clear position. "Do this, not that." | Hedging, "it depends" with no follow-up, both-sides padding |
| **Specific** | Marks, papers, syllabus codes, real mistakes, a line from an actual answer | "Study smart", "manage your time", "stay consistent" |
| **Funny** | Self-mocking, exaggerated, conversational. Asides in brackets. Sarcasm about situations everyone shares | Jokes at a person, a place or the reader. Forced jokes. A joke in every sentence |
| **Current** | Knows what students are doing, watching and saying. Uses it lightly and correctly | Slang in every line, forced references, a teacher doing an impression of a teenager |
| **On your side** | Assumes the reader is capable and busy. Explains without talking down | Lecturing, scaring, flattering, selling in every paragraph |

When two traits pull against each other, the order above decides. Straight beats funny. A joke never delays or blurs the answer.

## 4. Who is reading

| | Student | Parent |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | An exam is coming, or a decision is (subjects, resits, private candidacy) | Paying for it, worried about it, comparing options |
| Reads on | A phone, often late, often mid-revision | A phone, between other things |
| Wants | The method, fast. Proof you know the paper | A straight answer, costs and consequences, no jargon |
| Hates | Being told to "work hard" | Being sold to. Being made to feel behind |
| Humour that lands | The exam system, examiner logic, revision habits everyone has | School WhatsApp groups, relatives with opinions, results-day rituals |

Rules:

- Pick one reader per post and write to that one person as "you".
- Assume they are in Pakistan and sitting Cambridge exams. Do not explain what an O Level is. Do explain anything a first-time parent would not know (IBCC, components, series, thresholds).
- Never write to both readers in the same paragraph.

## 5. Who is speaking

| Post type | Narrator | Byline |
|---|---|---|
| Exam technique, mark scheme, model answers, subject guides | A named teacher, first person ("I") | The teacher who teaches that subject at SJ Academy |
| Opinion and takes | A named author, first person | The person whose opinion it is |
| System guides (IBCC, equivalence, registration, subject choices, fees for exams) | "We", SJ Academy | SJ Academy, reviewed by a named person |
| Reviews and buying guides (laptops, apps) | First person, when the author has actually used the thing. "We" for a roundup nobody tested | The author, or SJ Academy, with the affiliate disclosure |
| Entertainment posts (relatable lists, rankings, "types of" posts) | "We" | SJ Academy |
| News and updates (dates, syllabus changes, announcements) | "We", close to neutral | SJ Academy |

Rules:

- **A first-person claim must be true of the person named.** "I have marked this question for years" only appears if that teacher says so. An AI model never invents a teacher's experience; it leaves a bracket: `[teacher to add: a real example from marking]`.
- One narrator per post. Never switch between "I" and "we".
- Every post ends with a two-line author note: who wrote or reviewed it, and what they teach.
- "We" means SJ Academy's teachers. It is never the royal we of a company talking about its values.

## 6. The dial: how the voice shifts by post type

| Post type | Humour | Opinion | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entertainment posts | Full | Playful | The humour is the point. General and relatable. See section 9 |
| Opinion and takes | Full | Strong, labelled as opinion | About ideas and habits, never about a named place or person. Still factual |
| Exam technique | Regular | Strong on method | Jokes sit around the instruction, never inside it |
| Subject and decision guides | Regular | Clear recommendation | A recommendation is required. "It depends" must be followed by "on these two things" |
| Reviews and buying guides | Regular to full | Clear pick | Told as a real decision: what I doubted, why I chose, what annoys me. Never fake hands-on use |
| System guides (IBCC, equivalence, admissions) | Light | Minimal | Accuracy first. One dry line in the intro at most. Dated and sourced |
| News and updates | None to light | None | Facts, date, what to do next |
| Anything about failing, resits after a bad result, stress, money trouble, paper leaks that hurt students | None | Supportive and practical | Not the place for sarcasm |

## 7. Humour and sarcasm: the rules

Humour is a feature of this voice. The rule that governs all of it:

**General and relatable. Never a named place, organisation or person.** The joke is about a situation every student or parent recognises, so everyone can laugh and nobody is the punchline.

### The test

A humorous line stays only if it is all four:

1. **True.** It exaggerates a real thing.
2. **Relatable.** Most readers have lived it. They think "that is exactly what happens".
3. **General.** No school, academy, board, company, city, area or individual is named or obviously meant.
4. **Tasteful.** You would say it in class with parents in the room.

If it fails one, cut it. A post with no joke is fine. A post with a weak joke is worse than both.

### What the humour is about

| In range | Out of range |
|---|---|
| Exam season as an experience: deadlines, timetables, the night before | Any named school, college, academy, competitor or university |
| The way mark schemes and exam questions are worded | Cambridge, IBCC or any other organisation as the butt of the joke |
| Study habits everyone has (highlighting everything, "I'll start Monday") | Any individual: examiner, teacher, staff member, student, parent |
| Classroom and tuition moments everyone has seen | The reader's intelligence, effort or grades |
| Family situations everyone shares (the relative with opinions, the group chat) | Anyone who failed, resat or struggled |
| Ourselves: teachers, and tuition centres including this one | Religion, ethnicity, gender, class, accent, area or school background |
| Bad advice that goes round every exam season | Mental health. A family's money |
| Everyday life in Karachi and Pakistan that every reader shares | Politics. Stereotypes about an area, a community or a kind of school |

**When a joke has to point at something.** Sometimes the humour needs an object, for example the wording of a question type or a rule students find baffling. Then:

- Point at the thing, not the people behind it. "The mark scheme", not the board that wrote it.
- Keep it affectionate. The tone is "we all go through this", never "they are fools".
- One line, then move on. Do not build a paragraph on it.
- If you are unsure whether it is tasteful, it is not. Cut it.

**Naming organisations in general.** Posts name Cambridge, IBCC, universities and schools when stating facts the reader needs. That is information, and it is written straight. Humour and named organisations do not appear in the same sentence.

### Karachi and Pakistan flavour

The readers live here, so the humour should too. Use local detail wherever it is the natural way to say something.

In range, because everyone shares it:

- Load-shedding, the UPS, the generator, the internet dropping mid-lecture
- Traffic, and the time it takes to get anywhere
- Relatives who ask about grades, results-day phone calls, comparison with a cousin
- Chai, weddings in exam season, the cricket match that falls on the night before a paper
- Tuition culture: back-to-back classes, the last-minute crash course, "Sir, one more extra class"
- The path everyone knows: Matric or O Levels, IBCC, university entry tests

Rules:

- **Shared situations, not groups.** Load-shedding happens to everyone. A joke about people from a particular area, community, city or type of school is a stereotype and is out.
- **Karachi is the setting, never the target.** Affection, not complaint.
- **Language.** The site is English only. Everyday words that are part of Pakistani English are fine without translation: chai, beta, rishta, mamu, load-shedding, Sir and Miss. Full sentences in Roman Urdu are not used. If a line only works in Urdu, find another line.
- **Ramadan, Eid and other occasions** appear as part of the calendar students revise around. They are never the joke.
- No politics, no politicians, no current-affairs commentary.
- Do not force it. A post about mark schemes does not need a biryani reference.

Examples:

> Load-shedding has a sixth sense for the exact moment you open a past paper.

> Results day: the one day relatives you have not heard from since Eid remember your number.

> The UPS gives you about 40 minutes. Use them on the question you have been avoiding.

### Mechanics that fit the voice

These come from the owner's own writing (section 2). In rough order of how often he reaches for them:

- **Self-deprecation.** The writer is the first joke. "I teach this for a living and I still had to check."
- **Exaggeration.** One absurd image for a plain point. "A timetable so detailed it needs its own timetable."
- **The aside in brackets.** A short comment on the sentence it sits in. One per section at most.
- **Things that talk back.** The past paper, the mark scheme, the alarm clock, the UPS: give it a line.
- **Confession, then the real answer.** Admit the true reason, then give the useful one.
- **Staccato.** Three or four full stops in a row, once per post, on the point that matters most.
- **The run-on, then the brake.** A deliberately breathless sentence, then a three-word line. Once per post.
- **The honest translation.** What the official wording says, then what it means.
- **Straight-faced praise.** Solemnly defend something everyone knows is a bad idea. See section 9.
- **Understatement.** Used less often than the others, for contrast.

Not the voice: puns for their own sake, references that only work this month, copied memes, "lol", sarcasm marked with italics or quotation marks to make sure the reader noticed, and the same favourite word three times in one post.

### Placement

- Never in the short answer at the top.
- Never inside a step, a number, a date, a requirement or a definition. A student will copy those into their notes.
- Never in the call to action.
- Good places: the intro, the line after a hard truth, the end of a section, one heading per post.
- Follow a sarcastic line with a straight one. The reader should never have to wonder whether you meant it.

### Volume

| Post type | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Entertainment posts | Every item, since that is the format |
| Opinion | About one line per 120 words |
| Technique, guides, listicles | One or two lines per section |
| System guides | One in the intro, one more at most |
| News | None, or one |

These are ceilings, not targets. Two strong lines beat six average ones.

### Examples

Works:

> The mark scheme calls it evaluation. Students call it the paragraph where you repeat your answer with "however" in front of it. It scores what you would expect.

> You can revise by highlighting the whole textbook. The textbook will look great.

> Every family has one relative who is certain A Levels were harder in his day. He is welcome to sit Paper 4.

> Your school says the mock went well. The mark scheme has not been informed.

Does not work, and why:

> If you still cannot do ratio analysis after this, maybe Business is not for you.

Aimed at the reader.

> [School name]'s teachers never finish the syllabus, which is great news for us.

Names a place. Out of range, however it is worded.

> Only [exam board] could write a question this confusing.

Points at an organisation instead of the shared experience. "Whoever wrote question 4 was having a day" makes the same joke about nobody.

> Failed your AS? Oops.

A joke at someone living through a bad result.

> Step 3: Write the date in the box, unless you enjoy pain.

A joke inside an instruction.

## 8. Gen Z awareness

Most student readers are 15 to 19. The blog should sound like it knows that.

**The principle: aware, not fluent.** This is a teacher who understands the reference and uses it once, correctly. It is not a teacher performing as a teenager.

### What awareness means

- **It knows how they study.** Past paper sites, YouTube at 2x speed, "study with me" videos, notes on a phone, group chats at 2am, a subreddit, AI tools for explanations.
- **It does not pretend otherwise.** No lectures about phones. No pretending AI tools do not exist. Say what they are good for and where they will cost marks.
- **It respects their attention.** Answer first. Say how long something takes. "This takes 20 minutes" earns more trust than "this is important".
- **It is honest about effort and odds.** They can tell when they are being managed.
- **It is self-aware.** It can admit when advice sounds like every other piece of advice, then be different.

### Slang rules

- Student posts only. Never in posts for parents, system guides or news.
- **Two slang terms per post at most.** One is usually better.
- Only terms that have been in common use for a year or more and that most students would understand without a search.
- Use it plainly. No quotation marks around it, no explanation after it, no stacking.
- Never in the short answer, a step, a definition or the call to action.
- Never for a serious subject: a failed exam, stress, money.
- **Slang dates fast.** Whoever is closest to the students checks the terms before publishing, and old posts are not updated just to refresh slang. A post with none will still read well in two years; a post full of it will not.

### References

A reference to how students live is welcome when it is general: the group chat, the reel that promised an A* in 10 minutes, the timetable made on a Sunday night. A comparison to someone or something almost everyone knows is fine, the way a boxer or a famous reviewer might be mentioned in passing, as long as they are the comparison and never the target. Avoid this month's creators, shows, songs or trends. They date, and they turn a shared joke into one only part of the room gets.

### Examples

Works:

> If you are reading this the night before Paper 2, you are cooked on content. You are not cooked on technique. That part we can fix by morning.

> Yes, you can ask an AI tool to explain elasticity. It will do it well. It will also write you an answer that sounds impressive and misses the context, which is where the marks are.

> This takes 20 minutes. Put the phone face down. It will survive.

Does not work:

> No cap, this evaluation hack is bussin fr fr.

Stacked slang, and it is the writer performing.

> We know you kids love your TikToks, but exams are serious.

Talks down, and sounds like the relative from section 7.

> Stay locked in, king. The grind never stops.

Sounds like a caption, not a teacher.

## 9. Entertainment posts (the BuzzFeed style)

Posts written to be recognised, laughed at and shared. They bring in readers who were not searching for anything, and they show the brand has a personality.

- **For students only.** Parents can appear in them as characters, affectionately, but the reader is always a student.
- **One entertainment post for every five guides.** The blog is a resource first.

### Formats

| Format | Title pattern |
|---|---|
| Relatable list | "17 things every A Level student says the week before exams" |
| Types of | "The 7 types of students in every tuition class" |
| Ranking or tier list | "O Level subjects ranked by how much sleep they cost you" |
| Expectation and reality | "My revision timetable: the plan and what happened" |
| Translated | "What your parents say during exam season, and what they mean" |
| Stages | "The 6 stages of opening a past paper you have not revised for" |
| Signs | "11 signs you have been doing past papers for too long" |
| This or that, polls | "Past papers first or notes first: pick a side" |
| Straight-faced piece | "In defence of starting revision the night before" |
| Quiz | "Which A Level subject are you?" A quiz is built as a tool page, not a blog post |

### How they are written

- **Narrator:** "we". Playful, quick, warm.
- **Title:** a number, the group, the shared experience. It may tease, and the post must deliver exactly what it promised. No "you won't believe", no "number 7 will shock you".
- **Intro:** one or two lines. No short answer block; there is no question to answer.
- **Items:** a bold line that is the thing itself, then one to three sentences. Often the bold line is something people say, and the sentence under it is the dry truth.
- **Count:** 7 to 21 items. Odd numbers read as less manufactured. Cut any item that is filler.
- **Order:** strong start, strongest item last, the weakest ones cut rather than hidden in the middle.
- **Recognition over cleverness.** The best items make the reader tag a friend because it is them.
- **End:** one line, then one useful link that fits the topic. A list about past papers ends with the past papers page. No hard sell.

### The straight-faced piece

A whole post that solemnly argues for something every reader knows is a bad idea. It is the owner's most distinctive format.

- The subject is a habit or a situation, never a named place, organisation or person.
- Commit fully. No winking, no "just kidding" in the middle.
- It must be obvious by the second paragraph. If a tired student could take it as real advice, it is not obvious enough.
- Mark it in the post category as "Humour".
- **End with one straight line that gives the real advice,** and a link to the guide that covers it properly. A tuition centre cannot leave bad study advice hanging, even as a joke.

### Taste rules for this format

- Every rule in section 7 applies. General and relatable. No named place or person.
- **"Types of" posts describe behaviour, never identity.** "The one who asks if it is in the syllabus" is fine. A type defined by school, area, wealth, gender, accent or background is not.
- Every type should be one the reader can admit to being. If a type is only an insult, cut it.
- Include ourselves. At least one item where the teacher or the tuition centre is the joke.
- Rankings of subjects are about the experience of studying them, not their worth or the people who take them.
- Parents are characters, not readers, in these posts. They are laughed with, and the tone stays affectionate.
- Local flavour and emoji are both at home here. See sections 7 and 10.
- **No copied memes, GIFs or screenshots** from other creators or shows. Use our own graphics in the notebook style.
- Any fact stated in passing is still true and still checkable.

### Sample

> **9 things every A Level student says the week before exams**
>
> You have said at least six of these. Your teacher has heard all nine, every year, usually on the same day.
>
> **1. "I work better under pressure."**
> You have never tested the alternative.
>
> **2. "The threshold will be low this year."**
> Source: a voice note from someone's cousin. 💀
>
> **3. "I'll just do past papers."**
> Opens one past paper. Reads the mark scheme. Agrees with it. Closes the laptop.
>
> **4. "Will this come in the exam?"**
> Everything comes in the exam. That is what the exam is.
>
> **5. "I'm watching the lecture at 2x speed."**
> Now confused twice as fast.
>
> **6. "We covered this in class?"**
> We did. You were there. We have the attendance sheet.
>
> **7. "The light went, so I couldn't revise."**
> The light went at 9. You stopped at 7. 👀

## 10. Sentence craft

- **Short sentences, mixed with medium ones.** Most sentences under 20 words. Vary the length so it does not read like a telegram.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences. A one-line paragraph is a punch; use it at most once per section.
- **Second person.** "You", not "students" or "one".
- **Active voice.** "Examiners award the mark for", not "the mark is awarded for".
- **Contractions are fine** in first-person posts. Use fewer in system guides.
- **Plain words.** Use, help, show, start, about, so, but. Not utilise, facilitate, demonstrate, commence, regarding, therefore, however at the start of every sentence.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** Replace "improve your exam technique" with the technique.
- **Numbers as digits** for marks, dates, fees and counts. "8 marks", "3 papers".
- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two commas and an "and", split it. The one exception is the deliberate run-on in section 7.
- **Questions sparingly.** At most one rhetorical question per section, and answer it at once.
- **No em dashes.** Use a full stop or a comma.
- **Exclamation marks: two per post at most.** For a real verdict or real delight, never to prop up a joke, never two together. None in system guides, news or posts for parents.
- **No swearing,** including the mild kind.
- **One use per post** for any strong word: insane, obsessed, brutal, wild.
- **Say when there is nothing to say.** "There is not much to add here" beats a padded section.
- **Emoji** only where they add value. See the rules below.
- **British spelling:** enrol, practise (verb), organise, humour. Two exceptions: the logo tagline "Center of Excellence", and search phrases people actually type, such as "coaching center in Karachi", inside titles and headings.
- **Sentence case** for titles and headings.

### Emoji

Emoji are allowed where they add something the words do not: a reaction, a tone, a beat of timing.

**The test:** delete the emoji. If the line reads exactly the same, it was decoration. Leave it out.

| Where | Rule |
|---|---|
| Entertainment posts | Welcome. At most one per item, at the end of the line |
| Student guides and technique posts | Sparing. Up to three in a post, on the humorous lines |
| Opinion posts | Rare. One or two at most |
| Posts for parents, system guides, news | None |

Never:

- In the title, the short answer, a step, a number, a date, a definition or the call to action.
- In place of a word. The sentence must read correctly with the emoji removed, including for someone using a screen reader, which reads the emoji's name aloud.
- As bullet points, section markers or icons.
- In strings of two or more.
- On a serious subject.
- To signal that a line was a joke. If the line needs that, rewrite the line.

Use the common ones students already use for the reaction you mean. Avoid obscure ones and anything whose meaning shifts by group.

## 11. Structure of a post

| Part | Rule |
|---|---|
| Title | Says what the reader gets. Keyword near the front. Under 60 characters where possible. Sentence case. No "ultimate guide". No promise the post does not keep |
| Short answer | The first thing after the title. Two to four sentences that fully answer the question. No humour. Written so it makes sense quoted alone |
| Intro | Under 80 words. Starts in the reader's situation, not with background. This is where the first dry line goes |
| Body | H2 sections that each make one point. Headings are a claim or a question the reader would ask, not a label. "Why your evaluation scores zero", not "Evaluation" |
| Evidence | Each main point has one of: a mark scheme reference, a marked example, a number with a source, an official link |
| Takeaway | Three to five lines: what to do next, in order |
| Call to action | One, after the takeaway. See section 14 |
| Author note | Who wrote or reviewed it, what they teach, date updated, syllabus years covered |

Entertainment posts follow the format in section 9 instead of this table.

Formatting:

- **Prose first.** Paragraphs carry the argument.
- Bullets only for true lists: steps, requirements, items to bring.
- Tables for comparisons of three or more things.
- Bold for the one phrase per section a skimmer must catch. Never whole sentences.
- No bold-label bullets down a whole post ("**Tip:** ...").
- Length follows the question. A technique post can be 700 words. A system guide can be 2,000. No padding to hit a count.

## 12. Openings and endings

Do not open with:

- "In today's world", "In today's competitive environment", "As we all know"
- A dictionary definition
- "Are you a student who...?"
- The history of the subject
- A compliment to the reader

Open with one of:

- The mistake: what most students do, and what it costs.
- The moment: where the reader is right now (results day, a mock back with a C, a form to fill).
- The claim: the position the post will defend.
- The number: one real figure that reframes the problem.

Do not end with "In conclusion", "To sum up", a motivational line, or a question asking the reader to comment. End on the takeaway and the next action.

## 13. Truth rules

The voice can be sharp only because the content is right.

- **Every factual claim has a source** a reader could check: the syllabus, a mark scheme, an examiner report, an official page (Cambridge, IBCC, a university), or SJ Academy's own records.
- **Official processes are dated.** "As of October 2026" plus a link to the official page. Review these posts each exam series.
- **SJ Academy's own numbers** come only from the facts register in the brand guidelines.
- **Never invent** a statistic, a quote, a student story, an examiner comment or a teacher's experience. If it would help and you do not have it, leave a bracket for a human to fill.
- **Samples are labelled "sample".** Real marked answers are used with consent.
- **Opinion posts are labelled "Opinion"** and argue about ideas and habits, not about a named place or person.
- **Naming Cambridge:** follow the brand guidelines. Syllabus code on first mention, "May/June 2027" for series, no implied affiliation.
- **Affiliate posts** carry this line above the first link: "Some links in this post are affiliate links. We may earn a commission if you buy, at no extra cost to you. It does not change what we recommend."
- **No guarantees.** Never promise a grade.
- If a fact and a joke conflict, the fact wins and the joke goes.

### When we get something wrong

Correct the post, and say so at the top with the date. Use the owner's own register for it: plain, first person plural, no excuses, a little self-aware. "We got the conversion wrong in the first version of this post. It is fixed now, and we have checked it against the official page." No spin, no blaming the source, no burying the note at the bottom.

## 14. Calls to action in posts

- One call to action, after the reader has the full answer.
- It connects to the post: a technique post offers the class where that technique is taught, or the related resource.
- Plain wording, no sarcasm, no urgency tricks: "If you want this marked on your own answers, book a free demo class."
- Resource and tool links inside the body are help, not selling, and are welcome where they fit.
- Never interrupt a how-to with an offer.

## 19. Editing checklist

Ten minutes, in this order.

**Truth**

- [ ] Every fact has a source or a bracket. No invented numbers, quotes or stories
- [ ] Official rules are dated and linked
- [ ] First-person claims are true of the named author

**Straight**

- [ ] The short answer fully answers the title
- [ ] Each section makes a point you could disagree with
- [ ] Cut the first paragraph and check whether the post got better. If it did, keep it cut

**Specific**

- [ ] No advice that would fit any subject or any exam
- [ ] Each main point has a mark, a paper, an example or a link

**Funny**

- [ ] Every humorous line is true, relatable, general and tasteful
- [ ] No joke names or points at a place, organisation or person
- [ ] None in the short answer, steps, numbers or the call to action
- [ ] Read it as a parent whose child just got a bad result. Nothing stings
- [ ] The writer is the joke at least once
- [ ] Removed the weakest joke

**Current**

- [ ] Two slang terms at most, none in parent posts, and someone close to the students has checked them
- [ ] No trend-of-the-month references
- [ ] It sounds like a teacher who gets it, not one performing

**Clean**

- [ ] No em dashes. Two exclamation marks at most. No swearing
- [ ] No strong word used twice
- [ ] Every emoji passes the delete test, and none sit in the title, short answer, steps or call to action
- [ ] Local references are shared situations, not stereotypes
- [ ] No words from the banned list
- [ ] One narrator, one reader
- [ ] One call to action, after the takeaway
- [ ] Author note, updated date and syllabus years present
