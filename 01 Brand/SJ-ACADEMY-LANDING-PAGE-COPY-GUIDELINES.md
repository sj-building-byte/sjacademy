## 0. Before you write: the page brief

The best copy is decided before the first line is written. Fill this in for every page. Fifteen minutes here saves a rewrite.

| Field | What goes in it | A Level Business example |
|---|---|---|
| **Page** | Title and address | A Level Business tuition, `/a-level-business-tuition/` |
| **Lead reader** | Who the hero speaks to (section 3) | Student, AS or A2 |
| **Their situation, in their words** | 2 or 3 phrases from reviews, DMs or search queries | "I know the content but lose marks", "how to structure analyse and evaluate" |
| **The want behind the want** | What they really buy | To stop guessing what the examiner wants |
| **The one big idea** | One sentence the whole page exists to prove | Marks are lost to answer technique, and technique can be taught |
| **Proof we have** | Ranked on the proof ladder (section 9) | Mark splits, sample answer lines, 3 real reviews, 8+ years of business experience |
| **Objections to answer** | From section 11 | Fee, online, too late, past papers alone |
| **Keywords to keep** | Old title, H1 and ranking queries (section 16) | A Level Business tuition, Business Studies, 9609, Karachi, online |
| **Where visitors come from** | Ads, search, brand search, other pages, and what each one promised (section 22) | Instagram ads, brand searches, the past papers page |
| **Page code** | For the WhatsApp reference (section 13) | ALB-AS, ALB-A2, ALB-Q |
| **Facts to confirm** | Anything not in the facts register | Mock count, Friday class start date |

**The one big idea is the most important line in the brief.** Every section either proves it or answers an objection to it. If a section does neither, cut it. If you cannot write the idea in one sentence, you are not ready to write the page.

## 1. The page in one paragraph

An SJ Academy landing page sounds like a calm teacher who has seen this exact situation many times. It starts where the reader is ("clueless in the lab", "two weeks before CAIEs", "I keep losing marks on evaluation"), says plainly what we do about it, answers the questions people ask first before they have to ask, proves every claim with something specific, and makes the WhatsApp message take ten seconds. It is warm in the body, dry in small doses, and never hyped. The reader should finish the page thinking "these people know exactly what my problem is", not "these people are good at marketing".

### The three tests

Every line on the page passes all three, or it goes.

1. **Would a student or parent say "that is exactly my situation"?** If the line could sit on any tuition centre's page, it fails.
2. **Could a sceptical parent check it?** A number, a name, a paper, a time frame, a real review. "Best", "expert" and "quality" cannot be checked, so they are not used.
3. **Does it move the reader one step closer to the WhatsApp message?** By answering a question, removing a doubt or showing the method. A line that only decorates goes.

## 2. Where this guide comes from

Five sources, studied in October 2026:

- **Copywriting that converts.** The landing pages and ad work of writers known for short, believable, high-converting copy. What they agree on is in section 4.
- **How competitors write.** Nine Karachi and online tuition pages.
- **How parents choose.** Karachi and Islamabad studies, press columns and student forums on picking a tuition centre.
- **Our Google reviews.** 108 of about 225 read in full.
- **Our Instagram DMs.** 10 readable enquiry threads.

### What the research found, in five lines

1. **Everyone else sounds the same.** "Best", "expert teachers", "personalised attention", "small batches", "affordable fees", with no numbers behind any of them. Being specific is the cheapest way to stand apart.
2. **Nobody else explains the problem in subject terms.** Our A Level Business page does ("answers that lose marks"). That is our edge, and every page should use it.
3. **Parents are suspicious, and they have reason to be.** Columns tell them to ask about class size, ask whether they will hear about problems before results day, distrust guaranteed results and check ratings on the live Google profile. Our copy answers those questions before they are asked.
4. **People ask practical questions first.** Fee, timings, online or in class, who teaches. In the DMs, the first message is 3 to 8 words long and usually about money. A page that hides these answers sends people to Instagram to ask, and many of those threads went quiet.
5. **Our reviews already hold our best copy.** Students describe the before ("clueless", "scared to ask", "thin ice") and the after ("felt familiar", "a piece of cake", "50 to 72 in past papers within 2 months") better than any headline we could invent.

## 3. Who you are writing to

Landing pages have two readers. The blog rule still holds: **never write to both in the same paragraph.** Each section on the page has one reader, set in section 6.

| | Student | Parent |
|---|---|---|
| **Arrives from** | Instagram ad, Google search, a friend's WhatsApp | Google search, the child sending the link, a relative's recommendation |
| **First questions** | Who teaches it? When? Online or in class? Is it too late? | How much? Will my child get attention? Will I hear if it is going wrong? Results? |
| **What they say they want** | Better marks, to understand the subject | Good results, a teacher they can trust, value for money |
| **The want behind the want** | To walk into the exam hall calm. To stop feeling lost. Not to be the one who asks the "basic" question | Not to waste a year or the money. To find out early if it is going wrong. To stop worrying |
| **Tone** | Closer, quicker, lighter. A teacher talking to them before class | Slower and plainer. Structure, fees, progress, who is responsible |
| **Humour** | A dry line per section, about the shared experience | Almost none. One gentle line in the parent block at most |
| **Never** | Tell them to "work hard". Make them feel behind | Sell at them. Make them feel they have left it too late |

**The want behind the want.** Students do not buy "tuition". They buy the moment the paper opens and it looks familiar. Parents do not buy "classes". They buy the end of the worry. Write the feature, then the moment it creates.

| They are buying | What they want underneath | Write it as |
|---|---|---|
| Past-paper practice | The exam to feel familiar | "By May, Paper 3 looks like something you have already done many times." `[use a real count if you have one]` |
| Marked answers | To know exactly what is wrong | "Every answer comes back marked against the mark scheme, so you see which line lost the mark." |
| A lab workshop | Not to freeze in the practical | "You will have done every experiment on the syllabus with your own hands before the exam." `[check scope]` |
| A good teacher | To be allowed to ask the basic question | "Ask the question you think is too basic. It usually is not." |
| (Parent) Classes | To know early if it is going wrong | "If your child starts slipping, the teacher will tell you. You do not have to wait for results day." |

### Who the reader is by page

| Page | Lead reader | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Homepage | Split by section | The hero speaks to the student. The parent block, FAQ and final band speak to the parent |
| O Level page | Parent, then student | Parents of 14 to 16 year olds decide and pay. Hero speaks to the parent |
| A Level page | Student, then parent | A Level students often choose the teacher themselves. Hero speaks to the student |
| A Level subject pages | Student | One parent block, one parent FAQ group |
| O Level subject pages | Parent in the hero if the subject is a "rescue" subject (Maths, Chemistry, Physics), otherwise student | Decide per page and write it in the brief |
| Lab practicals | Student, especially private candidates | No demo class: students join directly. The CTA is "Ask about the next batch" |

## 4. Seven principles

These are what high-converting copy has in common, rewritten for a tuition centre in Karachi.

### 4.1 Start from the reader's situation, not from us

The first line the reader sees describes their problem in their words. "We" does not appear in the hero. On the whole page, "you" appears at least twice as often as "we".

- Not: "SJ Academy offers comprehensive A Level Chemistry tuition."
- Yes: "Never understood qualitative analysis? Most students have not. It is the part of the paper that is easiest to fix." `[check the claim]`

### 4.2 Specific is believable

A number, a paper, a code, a time frame, a named teacher doing a named thing. A specific claim is checkable, and checkable claims get believed.

- Not: "Experienced faculty with proven results."
- Yes: "Sir Salman has taught A Level Maths for 12+ years, including at KGS, and has recorded 500+ solved past paper videos."

### 4.3 Every number sits against a baseline

A number on its own means nothing. Give it a time frame, a comparison or a scale.

| Alone | With a baseline |
|---|---|
| 250+ A*s | 250+ A*s in 8+ years |
| Rated 5.0 | Rated 5.0 on Google, from [count] reviews |
| 4,500+ students | 4,500+ students taught since [year] `[confirm year]` |
| 5-day workshop | Every required practical, in 5 days `[confirm]` |
| Rs. 10,500 a month | From Rs. 10,500 a month per subject. 10% off when you pay 3 months upfront |

### 4.4 Feature and benefit in one breath

Never list a feature and leave the reader to work out why it matters. Join them in one sentence, usually with "so you" or "so your child".

- "Weekly scored tests, so you find the gap in March, not in May." `[confirm frequency]`
- "Classes recorded for online students, so a missed class is not a missed topic." `[confirm before use]`
- "Past papers marked the way an examiner marks them, so you stop guessing what earns the mark."

### 4.5 Answer objections in the open

Every doubt a parent has gets its own short, visible answer: a section, a FAQ item or a line under the button. Do not hide them. An objection that is answered openly becomes proof. Section 11 has the full list.

### 4.6 Give something useful before you ask

The page should teach something even if the reader never books. One real mark-losing mistake explained, a before-and-after answer, a free past paper link, the IBCC calculator. This is the page-sized version of the free demo class: the reader gets a taste of the teaching before deciding.

### 4.7 Short, scannable, one idea at a time

Most readers are on a phone and will scroll before they read. Every section works for a skimmer who reads only the label and the tagline, and for a reader who reads everything.

## 5. The section formula

Every section on a landing page is built from the same three parts.

| Part | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Label** | 1 to 3 words. What the section is. Small, above the heading | "The problem" |
| **Tagline** (the H2) | 2 to 7 words. A stance, not a label. It works read alone | "You know the content. The marks still leak." |
| **Body** | 2 to 3 sentences. The tagline's proof or explanation. One idea | "Most Business answers lose marks in the same three places: a generic point, no link to the case, and evaluation that only repeats the answer. All three are habits, and habits can be changed." |

Then, if the section needs it, a list, cards or a table. But the label, tagline and body must work on their own.

### Rhythm

- **Fragments are allowed** in taglines and short lines. "Marked. Explained. Fixed."
- **Parallel structure carries rhythm.** "Learn it in class. Practise it under time. See where the marks went."
- **Staccato once per page,** on the line that matters most.
- **Never chop a reason off its point.** If the second sentence explains the first, join them with "because" or "so". "Writing more does not fix it, because examiners are not paid by the page" sounds like a teacher. "Writing more does not fix it. Examiners are not paid by the page." sounds forced. Short sentences are for separate ideas, not for one idea cut in half.
- **Wordplay once per section at most, and never on a fact.** A pun can sit in a tagline. It never sits in a fee, a date, a timing, a requirement or a result.

### Lengths

| Element | Length |
|---|---|
| H1 | 4 to 9 words, plus the keyword pill |
| Hero subheading | 1 to 2 sentences, under 35 words |
| Section tagline (H2) | 2 to 7 words |
| Section body | 2 to 3 sentences, under 60 words |
| Card or list item | A bold line of 2 to 6 words, then 1 sentence |
| FAQ answer | 1 to 4 sentences |
| Button | 2 to 5 words |
| Handwritten note | Lowercase, 6 words or fewer |
| Whole page | Set by the sections, not a word count. A subject page with its syllabus detail and FAQ runs 1,500 to 2,100 words. The hero and the first two sections must work on their own |

## 6. Page anatomy, section by section

This follows the Enrol section order in the brand guidelines. Each section has a job, a reader, a formula and a line it must never cross.

### 6.1 Hero

**Job:** the reader knows in five seconds that this page is about their exact problem, and sees who teaches it, when, where and roughly what it costs.
**Reader:** set per page (section 3).

| Part | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Keyword pill** | The search phrase, plain: "A Level Maths tuition in Karachi & online". Never clever |
| **H1** | One of the headline patterns in section 7. The reader's situation plus a confident stance. One highlighted phrase |
| **Subheading** | Says plainly what we do: subject and code, teacher, where. No adjectives. "A Level Maths (9709) with Sir Salman. Pure, Mechanics and Statistics, in class at DHA Phase 7 or live online." |
| **Quick facts** | In the offset card. The first-call answers, in this order: teacher, timings (live from the timetable sheet), where (in class / live online), batch and start date, fee from. Each is one line. See section 12 for wording |
| **Primary button** | "Book a free demo on WhatsApp" |
| **Under the button** | One line that removes the last doubt, then a text link for visitors who are already sure: "One class free, then decide. Already sure? Register for a class." Fees and timetable links sit inside the card. Never a second big button |
| **Handwritten note** | Optional. One dry line next to the headline, lowercase, 6 words or fewer. "two points, done properly, beat six" |

**The hero must answer, without scrolling on a phone:** what subject, who teaches, online or in class, and how to book. Fee and timings go in the offset card, which can sit just below the fold.

**Never in the hero:** "we", "welcome", "best", a question the reader would answer "no" to, more than one joke, DHA Phase 7 in the subheading of a general page (the quick facts already carry it, and naming it there shuts out online students).

### 6.2 Proof strip

**Job:** three to four checkable facts, each with a baseline.
**Format:** a big number or short phrase, then a one-line baseline.

> **5.0 on Google** · from [count] reviews
> **250+ A*s** · in Maths, in 8+ years
> **12+ years** · teaching A Level Maths, including at KGS
> **Live online** · or in class at DHA Phase 7

Rules: facts only from the facts register or approved teacher claims. The Google rating links to the live Google profile. Parents are told to check it there, so make that easy. No humour in the strip.

### 6.3 The problem

**Job:** name the exact ways students lose marks in this subject, in their words. This is the section competitors do not have.
**Reader:** student.

- Label "The problem", then a tagline that names the pain: "You know the content. The marks still leak."
- Two to three specific mark-losing mistakes, each in one line, named in subject terms: "Generic points with no link to the case", "Evaluation that repeats the answer with 'however' in front of it", "Running out of time on the 12-marker".
- Use the students' own words from the reviews and DMs (section 10).
- End on the turn: these are habits, and habits can be fixed.

One dry line is welcome here. It is the most relatable section on the page.

### 6.4 Method (brown band)

**Job:** show how the teaching fixes the problem, as named steps.
**Reader:** student.

- 3 to 4 named steps. Each step has a short name, **one scene** (what actually happens in the room) and **one hard fact** (a frequency, a paper, a number).
- Name the steps as verbs the student does: "Learn it. Write it. Get it marked. Fix it."

> **Get it marked.** You write the answer under exam time. It comes back marked against the mark scheme, with the line that lost the mark circled. `[confirm turnaround]`

Never a vague step ("We focus on concepts"). If a step has no scene and no fact, it is not a step.

### 6.5 Teacher

**Job:** a named person the student will actually sit in front of, with checkable credentials and one thing he does that other teachers do not.
**Reader:** student (the parent reads it too, so keep it clean).

- Name, subject, credentials with baselines.
- **One named action**: "marks every experiment himself", "has solved 500+ past papers on video".
- One real review line about him, verbatim, if one exists.
- Subject pages do not link to the teachers page.

### 6.6 What you get

**Job:** the features, each joined to its benefit (principle 4.4).
**Format:** 4 to 6 cards or ticks. Bold line, then one "so you" sentence.

> **Past papers under exam time, then a full mock.** So May feels like a rerun, not a premiere. `[match the real mock count]`

Only list what is true for this class. No "doubt support anytime" (not promised). No class-size number.

### 6.7 For parents

**Job:** answer the parent's four questions in plain, slow copy: what it costs, how big the class is, how they will know if it is going wrong, and who is responsible.
**Reader:** parent. The only section addressed to "your child".

Approved wording to build from:

- **Cost:** "From Rs. [figure] a month per subject. 10% off when you pay 3 months upfront. Full fees on the fee page."
- **Attention:** "Classes are kept small, and attention is built into the routine: every student writes answers in class, every answer is marked, and every mock is scored." `[confirm each claim per class]` Never a number.
- **Progress:** "If your child starts falling behind, the teacher will tell you. You can ask for an update at any time." Do not promise reports on a schedule.
- **Responsibility:** the teacher's name, and the WhatsApp number as the line to the centre.

No humour in this block beyond one gentle line, and none at all about money.

### 6.8 Reviews

**Job:** let students say what we cannot say about ourselves.

- Three reviews, chosen by the proof ladder in section 9: before and after, a time frame, a paper or grade, a named teacher doing a named thing.
- Word for word. First name only. Spelling fixed only where it is plainly a typo; nothing else edited.
- A long review can be shortened to the part that matters, marked with "...", as long as the meaning does not change. Never join two reviews, and never reorder sentences.
- Under them: "SJ Academy is rated 5.0 on Google from [count] reviews. Read them on Google", linked to the live profile.

### 6.9 Something useful

**Job:** principle 4.6. One free resource that fits the page: past papers for the subject, a marked sample answer, the IBCC calculator.

> **Free:** A Level Maths past papers, sorted by paper and year.

### 6.10 FAQ

**Job:** every remaining first-call question and objection, in its own answer.

- 6 to 10 questions, ordered by the ranking in section 8.
- The question is written the way people actually ask it: "Is it too late to join?", not "Admission timelines".
- **Formula:** an optional dry line, then the concrete answer. The concrete answer must make sense on its own, because search engines quote it.

> **Is it too late to join?**
> Usually not, and we hear this question every month. [Concrete answer: when the current batch started, how missed topics are covered, when it genuinely is too late.] `[confirm]`

- At least two answers are for parents. Do not mix readers inside one answer.
- FAQPage structured data matches the visible text exactly.

### 6.11 Final band

**Job:** one last reason, one action.

- A short line that goes back to the reader's situation from the hero, then the button.
- "Sit one class before you pay for any." / "Bring the answer you lost marks on." `[only if the demo works this way]`
- One button. The main registration form on the homepage; WhatsApp demo on subject pages.

### Event pages (workshops, lab practical batches)

Same voice, different order: ticket hero (date, what, where, fee), what happens day by day, who it is for (private candidates by name), what to bring, FAQ, final band. Lab practicals have **no demo class**: the button is "Ask about the next batch on WhatsApp". The reviews for practicals are our strongest proof. Use them.

## 7. Headlines

### Patterns that work for us

| Pattern | Use when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Stop the costly habit** | The reader is losing marks to a known habit | "Stop writing Business answers that lose marks" |
| **Name the fear, then turn it** | The subject scares people | "Clueless in the lab? Good. That is where we start." |
| **The moment they want** | The want behind the want is clear | "Walk into Paper 3 knowing what is coming" |
| **The confident verdict** | A plain truth most students have not been told | "Maths is not talent. It is practice, marked." |
| **It is not too late** | Late joiners, crash courses, resits | "Two months to Paper 1 is enough. Here is how." `[only if true for the batch]` |
| **Plain and specific** | Parent-led pages, search-heavy pages | "O Level tuition in Karachi, taught by teachers from LUMS, IBA and KGS" |

### Rules

- Start from the reader, not from SJ Academy. No headline begins with "SJ Academy" or "We".
- A confident stance, not a question the reader can answer "no" to. "Struggling with Maths?" invites "not really" and the scroll.
- Highlight the phrase that carries the pain or the promise, never the keyword.
- Never promise a grade. "Get an A*" is out. "Write the answer that earns the marks" is in.
- Sentence case. No exclamation marks. No emoji.
- If a headline is clever but a tired student would need to read it twice, it is not the headline.

### Craft: how a line gets sharp

A good line is rarely the first one written. These are the moves that turn an accurate line into one people remember.

| Move | How it works | SJ Academy example |
|---|---|---|
| **The two-beat line** | Two short halves that contrast or mirror each other | "Fewer points. More marks." / "Hands on. Nerves off." / "Live online. Never left out." |
| **Twist a phrase they know** | Start from a saying the reader already has in their head, then turn it | "Practice makes perfect" becomes "Practice, marked." |
| **Swap the adjective for the fact** | Every adjective is a promise. Replace it with the thing that proves it | "lots of practice" becomes "500+ past papers, solved on video" |
| **The number moment** | One number, big and alone, with a short line under it | **500+** / past papers, solved on video. Your question is probably one of them |
| **The scene, not the claim** | Show the moment instead of naming the quality | "patient teachers" becomes "Ask the question you think is too basic." |
| **Say what others will not** | Honesty is rare in this market, so it stands out | "Usually not too late. Here is when it is." |
| **Cut until it breaks** | Remove words until the line stops working, then put one back | "Our teachers make sure every answer you write is checked carefully" becomes "Every answer you write gets marked." |

**The process:**

1. Write at least 10 options for the H1 and each tagline. The first one is almost never the best.
2. Run each through the three tests (section 1). Most will fail one.
3. Ask "so what?" of each survivor until the answer is something the reader feels. That answer is often the better line.
4. Read the last three aloud. Pick the one you would actually say to a student across a desk.

**What craft is not:** wordplay on a fact, a pun the reader has to decode, rhythm that hides what the class actually is, or a line that sounds good but could sit on any other tuition centre's page.

### Subheadings

The H1 can take a stance. The subheading has no stance at all: it says plainly what the class is, who teaches it and where. The H1 makes them stay. The subheading tells them they are in the right place.

## 9. Proof

### The proof ladder

Use the strongest proof you have. Higher on the ladder beats lower.

1. **Before and after, with a number and a time frame.** "50 to 72 in past papers within 2 months."
2. **A named paper or grade.** "An A in A Level Maths." "Paper 3 felt like a piece of cake."
3. **A named teacher doing a named thing.** "Sir Salman marks every experiment himself." `[confirm]`
4. **A physical or procedural detail.** "Every apparatus on the syllabus is in the lab." "Mocks are scored and handed back."
5. **The reader's type, served.** "Private candidate? Most of our practical students are." `[confirm]`
6. **Credentials with a baseline.** "12+ years at KGS."
7. **A rating with its count and a link.**
8. **An adjective.** Not proof. Not used.

### Rules

- **Facts only from the facts register** and approved teacher claims. If a number is not there, it does not go on the page.
- **Reviews are word for word,** first name only, and linked to the live Google profile.
- **The A* total is an estimate.** Do not lead with it, and do not put it on every page. A teacher-level figure with a baseline ("250+ A*s in 8+ years") is stronger and is approved.
- **The Google rating:** "SJ Academy is rated 5.0 on Google from [count] reviews", linked to the live profile. The rating covers the whole academy, so say so on subject pages. Never "226+ five-star reviews".
- **Never claim:** a class size number, one-on-one or home tuition, demos for practicals, doubt support outside class, scheduled progress reports, guaranteed grades, unconfirmed frequencies.
- **Business, Economics and Accounting pages are thin on proof.** Their reviews praise the teacher but give no grades or papers. Until that changes, these pages lean on rung 4 (show the method: a marked answer, the mark-losing mistakes) and on giving something useful. Collect grade-and-paper reviews from these students first.

### Words that are not proof

Competitors use these without numbers. We do not use them at all: **best, top, leading, number one, expert, experienced faculty, highly qualified, quality education, personalised attention, individual attention** (as a claim), **proven results, result-oriented, affordable, state-of-the-art, conducive environment.**

"Small batches" is allowed only when the next sentence shows how attention actually works.

## 10. Using the students' own words

The reviews describe the problem and the result better than we can. Use their words in the problem section, the method and the FAQ. Do not put them in quotation marks unless they are a real quote.

| Before joining | What changed | How they describe the teacher |
|---|---|---|
| clueless, no clue what to do in the lab | felt familiar | patient (the most repeated word) |
| never understood qualitative analysis | a piece of cake | chill, friendly, funny, like a friend |
| studying on my own has been challenging | didn't feel anxious | strict, because he sees the potential |
| 4 days before, two weeks before CAIEs, thin ice | questions very similar to what we practised | answered every question, even basic ones |
| intimidating, scared to ask | in 5 to 6 days, in a week and a half | on track with the syllabus |
| I wasn't expecting much | never returned home with a query unresolved | exam-oriented, past papers |
| I've always hated chemistry | stayed 3 to 4 years | mentor, motivated me to study |

How to use them:

- **The problem section** uses the "before" column. "Scared to ask the basic question at school? Ask it here."
- **The method and what-you-get** sections use the "what changed" column.
- **The teacher section** uses the third column, through real quotes.
- **"Patient" is our word.** It is what students say most about us. Show it rather than claim it: "Ask the question you think is too basic."
- Language people search and type: "O levels", "AS", "full course", "fee", "online or physical", "private candidate". Use these in FAQ questions and pills, exactly as typed. Use the correct form ("O Level") in body copy.

## 14. Humour on landing pages

The blog voice guide sets the rules for what humour is about (general, relatable, never a named target). On a landing page, the dial is turned down.

| Section | Humour |
|---|---|
| Hero H1 | Only if the stance itself is the wit ("Clueless in the lab? Good."). Never a pun |
| Hero subheading, quick facts, proof strip | None |
| The problem | One dry line. The best place for it |
| Method | One, around a step, never inside it |
| Teacher | Only through a real review |
| What you get | One, in a "so you" line at most |
| Parent block | None, or one gentle line. Never about money |
| FAQ | One dry line before the concrete answer, in at most a third of the answers |
| Buttons, WhatsApp message, fees, timings, dates | None |
| Handwritten notes | The main home for wit. Max one per section, lowercase, 6 words or fewer |

**The rule from the strongest copywriters:** humour wraps around facts and never replaces them. A reader who skips every joke still gets every answer.

Examples that fit:

> Load-shedding is not on the syllabus. We plan around it anyway. `[only on a page with online classes or recordings]`

> The mark scheme has opinions. We teach you what they are.

> Yes, the relative who asked about your grades will ask again. This time you will have an answer.

> Ask the question you think is too basic. Someone else in the room was about to.

Does not fit on a landing page, even if it would on the blog: anything sarcastic about the reader's current marks, any joke about fees, any slang in a parent section, emoji in the hero or the CTAs.

## 15. Sentence craft

Everything in section 10 of the blog guide applies: short sentences, active voice, plain words, digits, British spelling, sentence case, no em dashes, no swearing. On landing pages, also:

- **"You" at least twice as often as "we".**
- **No exclamation marks** anywhere on a landing page. Confidence does not need them.
- **Emoji:** none in the hero, headings, proof, fees, buttons or WhatsApp message. One or two at most on the page, in a handwritten note or a student FAQ, and only if they pass the delete test.
- **Slang:** student sections only, one term per page at most, and zero is better. None on O Level parent-led pages.
- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence carries two features, split it. A reason is not a second idea: keep it joined with "because" or "so" (section 5).
- **Contractions are welcome** in student sections ("you'll", "it's"). Fewer in the parent block.
- **No rhetorical question without an immediate answer.**
- **Read the page aloud.** If a line sounds like a brochure, rewrite it as the teacher would say it across a desk.

## 16. Search

The rebuild must not lose rankings. Copy and search want the same things.

- **Before rewriting,** record the page's current title, H1, meta description and ranking queries (from Semrush or Search Console). The new copy keeps those keywords.
- The keyword sits in the **pill inside the H1**, the subheading, one H2 and the first 100 words, in natural wording.
- Search phrasing stays as typed: "coaching center in Karachi", "A Level tuition in Karachi". British spelling elsewhere.
- **Write one plain, complete factual sentence** for each thing a search engine or AI assistant might quote: "SJ Academy teaches A Level Business (9609) in Karachi at DHA Phase 7 and live online, with Sir Sunyyan Junaid." Keep jokes beside these sentences, never inside.
- FAQ questions use the wording people search. FAQPage data matches the visible text exactly.
- Meta description: the situation, what we do, the proof, the action, under 155 characters. "Losing marks on 9609 evaluation? A Level Business tuition in Karachi & online with Sir Sunyyan Junaid, AS and A2. Book a free demo class."
- SEO title: the keyword first, under 60 characters, no promised grade. "A Level Business tuition in Karachi & online | SJ Academy". Set the Yoast social title to match, because that is what WhatsApp shows when a link is shared.

## 22. Ads and message match

Most new visitors arrive from an Instagram ad. The fastest way to lose them is a page that does not match what the ad said.

- **One ad, one page.** An ad for A Level Business lands on the A Level Business page, never the homepage.
- **Same promise, same words.** The first screen repeats the ad's main claim and offer in the same words. If the ad says "free demo class", the hero button says it too.
- **Same reader.** An ad aimed at parents lands on a page whose hero speaks to parents.
- **Tag every ad link** with `utm_source=instagram&utm_campaign=[campaign]&utm_content=[ad name]`, so the page's clicks can be traced back to the ad.
- **Cold and warm visitors read differently.** Someone from an ad does not know us yet, so the proof strip and the problem section do the work. Someone who searched "sj academy" already trusts us, so the hero card and the register link do the work. A good page serves both without changing.

## 23. Measure and improve

A page is never finished. It is the current best guess, checked against what people actually do.

| What to watch | Where | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| WhatsApp clicks per 100 visits | GA4, `whatsapp_click` events by page and `data-loc` | Whether the page persuades, and which button does the work |
| Enquiries with this page's code | The lead tracker, by `(Ref: ...)` | Whether clicks become real conversations |
| Enrolments from those enquiries | The lead tracker | Whether the page brings the right students |
| Click-through rate in search | Search Console, filtered to the page | Whether the title and meta description earn the click |
| Questions people still ask | The team's WhatsApp chats | What the page failed to answer |

**How to improve a page:**

1. Review each page once a month.
2. Every question people still ask after reading the page becomes an FAQ answer or a hero line.
3. Change one thing at a time, in this order: the H1, the line under the button, the hero card, the proof strip. Give each change 4 weeks, because traffic is small and a week proves nothing.
4. Record each change and its date in the site register, so a result can be traced to a cause.
